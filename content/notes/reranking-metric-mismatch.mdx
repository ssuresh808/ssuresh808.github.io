When I built the retrieval layer for my [Agentic RAG system](/projects/agentic-rag/), I expected a cross-encoder reranker to be the easy win. Reranking is the usual step after hybrid search. On my benchmark it made the headline number worse, and this note is about why.

## The setup

The benchmark is rag-mini-wikipedia: 3,200 passages indexed, 300 factoid questions, with yes/no and very short answers excluded. Relevance is scored by answer-string containment, meaning a retrieved passage counts as relevant if it literally contains the gold answer string. That proxy is cheap and needs no human labels, which is why I started with it.

I compared three configurations: dense retrieval alone with bge-large embeddings, dense plus BM25 hybrid fusion, and hybrid plus a bge-reranker-base cross-encoder that fetches twenty candidates and keeps the top five.

| Configuration | Hit@5 | MRR | Recall@5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dense (bge-large) | 0.697 | 0.594 | 0.487 |
| Dense + BM25 hybrid | 0.717 | 0.639 | 0.496 |
| Hybrid + cross-encoder rerank | 0.690 | 0.520 | 0.470 |

Hybrid beat dense by 0.045 MRR. That part matched expectations. BM25's exact-term matching catches the date, name, and quantity questions where dense embeddings blur the one token that matters.

Then reranking dropped MRR from 0.639 to 0.520 and came in lower on every other metric in the table too, so this was not noise.

## What the reranker was optimizing

My first instinct was a bug, so I checked the wiring: the candidate pool was right, the scores were being applied, and the top five were the reranker's top five. Everything worked as designed, so I looked at the losing cases one at a time.

The pattern was consistent. For a question like "when did X happen", the reranker promoted passages that discussed X in depth and demoted the passage that happened to contain the four-digit year. The cross-encoder is trained for semantic relevance: does this passage address this question? The metric rewards something narrower: does this passage contain the answer string? Those two objectives agree most of the time. They disagree on the passages a reranker is built to surface: topically relevant ones that paraphrase or summarize the answer without containing the literal string.

So the reranker was working. The metric was grading it on something it was not trained to do. With an LLM judge or gold passage labels I would expect a different result, but I have not run that comparison yet, so I do not claim it.

## What I did with the result

I shipped hybrid retrieval as the default and left the reranker as an opt-in flag. I wrote the finding down as an architecture decision record with the numbers, the losing cases, and the follow-up plan, rather than removing the reranker or leaving the result out of the docs.

The follow-up is a relevance-aware evaluation: either gold passage labels for a sample of questions or an LLM judge that scores whether a passage answers the question rather than whether it contains a string. Until that exists, the reranker stays off, because the only evidence I have says it hurts the thing I can measure.

## What I took from it

The metric I chose decided which technique won. Answer-string containment is a reasonable first metric for retrieval, but it favors lexical methods like BM25 and penalizes anything that matches on meaning. If I had only read the table, I would have concluded that reranking does not work. Reading the failures showed that the metric was measuring the wrong thing for this comparison.

When an established technique loses on a benchmark, I now check the benchmark before I blame the technique, and I write down which one I decided to trust and why.

The numbers, the configuration flags, and the decision record are in the [repository's experiments and decisions docs](https://github.com/ssuresh808/Agentic-RAG-System-over-LLM-Inference-Optimization-Literature/blob/main/docs/experiments.md).
